Build an investigation

  1. Make two cards. Check that they have just one thing in common.
  2. Add a third. It must work with both earlier cards.
  3. Can you reach four cards? Predict which symbols your next card needs.
  4. Keep going. If you get stuck, change an earlier card. Record what your change fixes—and what it breaks.
A small hint

Try keeping one symbol on several cards. What must be different about all their other symbols?

A bigger hint

For the seven-card challenge, try using each symbol on three cards. This alone does not guarantee success: keep testing every pair.

Further: why seven?

A card with three symbols can meet six other cards, if each symbol is on three cards altogether: 3 × 2 = 6. Including itself gives seven. Explain why no other card can be counted twice.

More generally a finite projective plane of order q gives q² + q + 1 cards, q + 1 symbols per card, and one shared symbol per pair. Such planes exist when q is a prime power; they are not guaranteed for every whole number. The seven-card case has q = 2.

Educator notes · a practical session

Use blank cards and removable stickers alongside the screen. Begin with two symbols per card and three available symbols. Ask “Can you make three cards that all work?” Move to three symbols per card when the rule is secure.

For a design-first learner: offer a choice of symbols, alternate making and testing, and treat a failed pair as information. For a learner ready to justify patterns: predict the next card before making it, keep a table of symbol counts, and justify a maximum under the stated restrictions.

Suggested hour: 5 minutes matching; 10 making three small cards; 20 building and revising a larger set; 10 testing systematically; 10 explaining a discovery; 5 playing with the deck. Adjust to engagement. The accessible matching game and the deeper proof have very different prerequisites.
