A mathematical animation, not a biological gait simulator.

Try three experiments

Open the session and experiment sheet →

  1. Make 10 segments. Predict the number of legs before counting.
  2. Compare a straightish route with a tight bend. What stays the same?
  3. Set the wave to 0°, then 90°, then 180°. Describe the movement without using numbers first.

Change one setting at a time. Pause to sketch or record a prediction.

Educator notes · a one-hour session

0–10 minutes: draw a creature and discuss what needs to repeat. Use paper circles and two pipe-cleaner legs per segment.

10–25: choose a route, build a creature, and compare predictions with the screen. Offer the choice of route or colour before adding numerical challenges.

25–40: investigate segments and legs with a small table. Ask “What changes when we add one segment?”

40–50: compare waves and bends; make a controlled experiment. 50–60: save a creation, explain one discovery, then choose a follow-up.

Design-first route: begin with drawing and design; use a short predict–try–describe cycle, with optional counting and a manageable finishing point. Avoid making the session depend on rapid arithmetic.

Reasoning extension: extend to 2n legs, equivalent totals, alternating leg phases, path length and constant speed. Ask for a reason, not just a pattern.

Maths underneath: positions are sampled by distance along a smoothed polyline. Each segment uses its own tangent and normal. The leg swing follows a sine wave with a phase offset. This original implementation follows the general arc-length idea discussed in the linked Mathematica discussion; it does not copy its code. The supplied worm1.nb and worm2.nb were inspected. The notebook-loop preset uses their golden-ratio-scaled sin(3t), sin(2t) route. Body taper and width controls reflect the notebook experiments. This browser model uses distance-based spacing and its own simplified leg model; it is not an exact execution of the Mathematica notebooks.
