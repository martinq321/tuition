Multiplication studio · Explore and connect

One multiplication.
Many ways to see it.

Split a rectangle. Move the split. What changes—and what stays the same?

×

Choose 2–40 in each row and 2–12 rows. Start small if that helps.

Notice and explain

Keep the same total number of counters. Move the split to three different places. Which calculation is easiest for you? Can you convince someone that no counters have been added or lost?

Try your strategy

Could another strategy help?

Make it on the table

Use counters, dried beans or squared paper. Make the array and lay a pencil across it to split it. Write a calculation for each part.

For doubling and halving, cut an even number of rows into two equal rectangles. Put them end to end. Explain why the area is unchanged.

Investigate further

Find three different splits that help you calculate 18 × 6. Which relies on facts you already know?

Someone says “24 × 6 = 20 × 6 + 4.” Use an array to explain what is missing.

When is a nearby ten more useful than splitting into tens and ones? Give two examples.

For parents and educators

Move between concrete objects, pictures and symbols, keeping the same example long enough to connect them. A learner may explain accurately before recalling multiplication facts fluently. Record those strengths separately.

Ask the learner to choose and justify a strategy. Do not require every method for every question. Follow exploration with a few independent examples and revisit later.

This original workshop supports Year 4 multiplication and division, particularly partitioning and using known facts. It is informed by White Rose's representational approach; it is not an official White Rose resource. It is an exploratory bridge towards written multiplication, not the complete block.

First attempts and hints are counted for this visit only. No timed scoring. Larger written multiplication and persistent learner assessment are still to develop.
